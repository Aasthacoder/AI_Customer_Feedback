# Product Problem Framework

## 1. Feedback Is Not the Same as a Problem

A customer complaint describes an experience, but the complaint itself may not be the underlying product problem.

For example:
> "I was charged a fee that I didn't expect."

This is feedback.

A possible underlying problem could be:
> "The customer did not understand when or why the fee would be charged."

The distinction matters because solving the literal request may not solve the actual customer problem.

---

## 2. Feedback → Theme → Problem → Opportunity

I use the following structure throughout the analysis:

**Feedback**
What did the customer say?

    ↓

**Theme**
What similar feedback appears repeatedly?

    ↓

**Problem**
What underlying customer difficulty appears to be causing the feedback?

    ↓

**Opportunity**
What could potentially be improved?

    ↓

**Product Solution**
What could be built or changed to address the opportunity?

---

## 3. Example

### Customer feedback

A customer complains that they were charged a credit-card fee they did not expect.

### Theme

Unexpected or unclear fees.

### Possible problem

Customers may not understand the conditions under which certain fees are charged.

### Opportunity

Improve the visibility and timing of fee-related information.

### Possible product solution

Provide clearer, contextual fee information before the customer reaches the point where the charge occurs.

This is only an example of the reasoning process. The actual problems used in the final roadmap will come from the data analysis.

---

## 4. Existing CFPB Categories

The dataset already contains fields such as Product, Issue, and Sub-issue. These provide a useful starting point for understanding complaints.

However, I will not assume that these categories represent the complete product problem space.

The analysis will investigate whether:

* Different categories contain similar underlying problems.
* One category contains several distinct problems.
* Customer language reveals patterns that are not obvious from the existing labels.

---

## 5. Problem Quality

A problem becomes more useful for product decision-making when there is evidence that it is:

* recurring,
* meaningful to customers,
* understandable,
* and potentially addressable through a product or service change.

The final analysis will therefore avoid treating every complaint as a separate product problem.

---

## 6. Product Opportunity

An opportunity represents a potential area for product improvement based on the problems found in the feedback.

For example:

> **Problem:** Customers struggle to understand why a particular charge occurred.

could lead to:

> **Opportunity:** Make important charges easier to understand before and after they occur.

The opportunity is intentionally broader than a specific feature.

This prevents the analysis from jumping directly from customer feedback to a predetermined solution.

---

## 7. Prioritization

A high-volume problem will not automatically become the highest-priority product opportunity.

Priority will eventually consider the evidence available around:

* customer impact,
* frequency,
* trend,
* confidence,
* potential product impact,
* and effort.

The exact prioritization framework will be developed after the customer problems have been discovered.
